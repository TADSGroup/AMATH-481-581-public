\chapter{Solving the Heat Equation with the Method of Lines}

In this lecture we take a close look at the method of lines for solving 
the 1D heat equation 
\begin{equation*}
    \left\{
\begin{aligned}
    & \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} 
    + f, \qquad (t, x) \in (0, 1] \times (0, 1), \\
    & u(0, x) = c(x), \qquad x \in(0,1), \\ 
    & u(t, 0) = u(t, 1) = 0, \qquad t \in [0, 1], 
\end{aligned}
    \right.
\end{equation*}
i.e., by turning our PDE into a system of ODEs. The 
method of lines for arbitrary time-dependent PDEs consists of the following steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Discretize the PDE in space using a method of choice, e.g., FDM, spectral, FEM, etc.
    \item Write down a system of ODEs for the coefficients describing the spatial discretization. 
    \item Solve the system of ODEs using an appropriate time integrator. 
\end{enumerate}

We will now describe these steps for our PDE. 

\subsection*{Discretize in space:}

We will use the finite difference method as in the previous lecture. 
Let us consider a uniform spatial grid, 
$x_m = m \Delta x$ for $m =0, \dots, M$ and $\Delta x = 1/M$. 
Since the right-hand side of the PDE only involves $u_{xx}$ we can employ 
the centered difference formula to write 
\begin{equation*}
    u_{xx}(t, x_m)  = \frac{u(t, x_{m-1}) - 2 u(t, x_m) + u(t, x_{m+1})}{\Delta x^2} 
    + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2). 
\end{equation*}
We also note that the Dirichlet boundary conditions imply that 
\begin{equation*}
    u(t, x_0) = u(t, x_M) = 0.
\end{equation*}
Thus, evaluating the PDE at the points $(t, x_m)$ we have the approximation 
\begin{equation*}
    \left\{
\begin{aligned}
    & \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t, x_m) =
    \frac{u(t, x_{m-1}) - 2 u(t, x_m) + u(t, x_{m+1})}{\Delta x^2} 
      + f(t , x_m) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2), \qquad t \in (0, 1], \\
    & u(0, x_m) = c(x_m), \qquad m = 1, \dots, M-1, \\ 
    & u(t, x_0) = u(t, x_M) = 0, \qquad t \in [0, 1], 
\end{aligned}
    \right.
\end{equation*}

\subsection*{Write down the semi-discrete PDE as a system of ODEs:}

Let us now introduce the functions $u_m: [0,1] \to \R$ such that 
$u_m(t) \approx u(t, x_m)$. Notice that, thanks to the boundary 
conditions, we immediately have $u_0(t) = u_M(t) = 0$. 
For the rest of the $u_m$'s we write, by dropping the $\mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)$ 
error terms, the system of ODEs 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
            u'_1 &= \frac{1}{\Delta x^2} (-2 u_1(t) + u_2(t) ) + f_1(t), \\ 
            u'_m &= \frac{1}{\Delta x^2} (u_{m-1}(t) - 2 u_m(t) + u_{m+1}(t)) + f_m(t), \quad 
            m = 2,\dots, M-2, \\ 
            u'_{M-1}& = \frac{1}{\Delta x^2} (u_{M-2}(t) - 2 u_{M-1}(t) ) + f_{M-1}(t),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
with initial condition $u_m(0) = c_m = c(x_m)$ and source functions $f_m(t) = f(t, x_m)$. Thus, we can rewrite this 
system of ODEs in matrix form as 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heat-eqn-semi-discrete}
    \ub'(t) = A \ub(t) + \fb(t), \qquad \ub(0) = \cb,
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation*}
    A = \frac{1}{\Delta x^2}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        -2 & 1 & & & \\
        1 & -2 & 1 & & \\
        & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \\
        & & 1 & -2 & 1 \\
        & & & 1 & -2
    \end{bmatrix} \in \R^{(M-1) \times (M-1)},
\end{equation*}
and $\ub(t) = (u_1(t), \dots, u_{M-1}(t))$,
$\fb(t) = (f_1(t), \dots, f_{M-1}(t))$,
and $\cb = (c_1, \dots, c_{M-1})$.

\subsection*{Solve the ODE system using an appropriate integrator:}
With the ODE system at hand, we can now use our favorite methods from Part I of the course 
to approximate the solution $\ub(t)$. For example, if we use forward Euler, we obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    \ub_{n+1} = \ub_n + \Delta t \left( A \ub_n + \fb_n \right), \qquad n = 0, \dots, N-1, 
\end{equation*}
where $\Delta t = 1/N$ as usual.  
Alternatively, we could have used any other method 
from Part I, such as Runge--Kutta or linear multistep methods, as well as implicit methods such as 
backward Euler, 
\begin{equation*}
    \ub_{n+1} = \ub_n + \Delta t \left( A \ub_{n+1} + \fb_{n+1} \right), \qquad n = 0, \dots, N-1.
\end{equation*}
Indeed the choice of the solver is a delicate matter in this step, due to the fact that 
the system \eqref{eq:heat-eqn-semi-discrete} can become very stiff. 

To see this, consider the simpler problem when $\fb = 0$ so that 
the ODE system is simply $\ub' = A \ub$.  
We note that the matrix $A$ is symmetric and negative definite, and so 
is diagonalizable. In fact, its eigenvectors and eigenvalues are
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{gathered}
    \qb_k = \big( \sin(k \pi x_1), \sin(k \pi x_2), \dots, \sin(k \pi x_{M-1}) \big), \\
    \lambda_k = -\frac{4}{\Delta x^2} \sin^2\left( \frac{k \pi \Delta x}{2} \right),
    \qquad k = 1, \dots, M-1,
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation*}
i.e., $(\qb_k)_m = \sin(k \pi m \Delta x)$. This can be verified directly using the 
sine identity
$\sin(\alpha - \beta) + \sin(\alpha + \beta) = 2 \sin(\alpha) \cos(\beta)$, which gives
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{(\qb_k)_{m-1} - 2 (\qb_k)_m + (\qb_k)_{m+1}}{\Delta x^2}
    = \frac{2 \cos(k \pi \Delta x) - 2}{\Delta x^2} (\qb_k)_m
    = -\frac{4}{\Delta x^2} \sin^2\left( \frac{k \pi \Delta x}{2} \right) (\qb_k)_m,
\end{equation*}
where the first and last rows are covered by the fact that
$(\qb_k)_0 = \sin(0) = 0$ and $(\qb_k)_M = \sin(k \pi) = 0$.
All eigenvalues are negative and they range between
\begin{equation*}
    \lambda_1 = -\frac{4}{\Delta x^2} \sin^2\left( \frac{\pi \Delta x}{2} \right) \approx - \pi^2
    \qquad \text{and} \qquad
    \lambda_{M-1} = -\frac{4}{\Delta x^2} \cos^2\left( \frac{\pi \Delta x}{2} \right) \approx - \frac{4}{\Delta x^2}.
\end{equation*}
Now recall that for forward Euler to remain absolutely stable for an ODE system we needed 
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta t \le \min_k \frac{2}{| \lambda_k | },
\end{equation*}
which in this case, for small $\Delta x$, gives the condition 
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta t \le \frac{1}{2} \Delta x^2.
\end{equation*}
In other words, as we refine the spatial grid $\Delta x$ the time step $\Delta t$ 
should be refined quadratically to obtain a stable solution.

\begin{quote}
    This is a very severe constraint that is inherited by other explicit solvers as well, 
    and is due to the fact that our ODE system \eqref{eq:heat-eqn-semi-discrete} is 
    very stiff. This behavior is also inherited by other parabolic PDEs and so 
    when solving such problems we try to avoid using explicit ODE solvers and rely on 
    implicit solvers.
\end{quote}

We demonstrate this behavior in \Cref{fig:heat-fe-vs-be} where we take $f = 0$,
$M = 50$ (so that $\Delta x^2/2 = 2 \times 10^{-4}$), and an initial condition $c(x)$
that is a mixture of a skinny and a wide Gaussian. Since the solution decays 
quickly in this case, we only solve the problem on the shorter time interval 
$t \in (0, 0.1]$ instead of $(0, 1]$. Backward Euler with
$\Delta t / \Delta x^2 = 2.5$ and forward Euler with $\Delta t / \Delta x^2 \approx 0.48$
produce essentially the same solution. However, increasing the forward Euler
time step only slightly, to $\Delta t / \Delta x^2 \approx 0.507$, leads to
a growing high frequency oscillation in the numerical solution. This is
precisely the threshold identified above.
Taking $\Delta t = 10^{-3}$, the time step used for backward Euler, forward Euler
blows up and reaches values of order $10^{90}$ by $t = 0.1$.

Finally, in \Cref{fig:heat-forced} we solve the problem with a nontrivial
source term $f(x)$ using backward Euler with an even larger time step, 
$\Delta t / \Delta x^2 = 5$, on the time interval $t \in (0, 0.5]$ (which is already
long enough for the solution to settle), and observe that
the solution converges to the steady state $u_\infty$, which solves the
BVP $-u_\infty'' = f$ with $u_\infty(0) = u_\infty(1) = 0$.

\chapter{Absolute stability}

In \Cref{lec:accuracy} we introduced the notions of convergence and numerical 
accuracy. In essence, a convergent numerical algorithm is the minimum we need 
for it to be useful since, 
eventually and with enough computing power (small $\Delta t$),
the method should give us a good approximation to the truth. Having an order 
of convergence makes this much stronger because it tells us precisely how 
fast the error decays. However, as it turns out accuracy alone is not 
quite sufficient for making a method useful and as we will see there is a
tension between accuracy and the idea of stability that we describe below. 

\section{Absolute stability}

Recall our test ODE \eqref{eq:test_ode} 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d u}{d t} = \lambda u, \qquad u(0) = c.
\end{equation*}
and in particular the experiment in 
\Cref{fig:test_ode_forward_backward_euler_comparison} where we 
saw that when $\Delta t$ is too large then the forward Euler method 
seems to oscillate wildly while backward Euler remains stable. This behavior 
is so fundamental that the mathematical definition of stability is 
often built around the test ODE itself. 

Recall that the test ODE has analytic solution $e^{t \lambda} c$ and so 
if $\text{Re}(\lambda) < 0$, then the solution decays to zero as $t \to \infty$.
Now if we apply forward Euler to this problem we obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    u_{n} = u_{n-1} + \Delta t \lambda u_{n-1} = (1 + \Delta t \lambda) u_{n-1}.
\end{equation*}
Substituting the same expression for $u_{n-1}$ recursively, we obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    u_n = ( 1+ \Delta t \lambda)^n u_0.
\end{equation*}
Thus, the sequence converges to zero if and only if 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forward_euler_stability_condition}
 |1 + \Delta t \lambda| < 1   
\end{equation}
 If $\lambda$ is real and negative, then 
\begin{equation*}
    -1 \le 1 - \Delta t \lambda \le 1 \quad \Rightarrow \Delta t \le  
    \frac{2}{-\lambda} 
    = \frac{2}{|\lambda|} =: \Delta t_{\max}.
\end{equation*} 
Thus we have a limit on the size of $\Delta t$ if we want the 
solution to decay to zero as $n \to \infty$. If $\lambda$ is complex then 
the condition \eqref{eq:forward_euler_stability_condition} corresponds
to the quantitiy $\Delta t \lambda$ belonging  
to the disk of radius $1$ centered at $-1$ as shown in 
\Cref{fig:euler-stability-region}.

You might wonder why do we care about this particular 
test problem and the decay of the solution to zero? Suppose 
we are solving an ODE numerically using forward Euler 
and have obtained a sequence of solutions $\{ u_{n} \}_{n =0}^{N-k}$ but a 
small perturbation is introduced in the last estimate 
$u_{N-k} \leftarrow u_{N-k} + \epsilon$. This happens all the time
due to numerical round-off errors.
Then the forward Euler method will continue to produce 
the rest of the sequence as 
\begin{equation*}
    u_{\epsilon,N-k+j} = (1 + \Delta t \lambda)^j u_{N-k} = (1 + \Delta t \lambda)^j (u_{N-k} + \epsilon), \quad j = 1, 2, \dots, k.
\end{equation*}
In other words, the error $\delta u_{N-k+j} = u_{\epsilon,N-k+j} - u_{N-k+j}$ 
satisfies
\begin{equation*}
    \delta u_{N-k+j} = (1 + \Delta t \lambda)^j \epsilon, \quad j = 1, 2, \dots, k.
\end{equation*}
and we prefer if this perturbation does not grow. 

You might also wonder why do we care only about the linear ODE?
 and how does this relate to "stability" of a solver in 
more complex and nonlinear problems? 
To see this suppose that we have a more general ODE 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d u}{d t} = f(u), \qquad u(0) = c,
\end{equation*}
with ground truth solution $u(t)$. 
Now suppose that we make some small errors, say in 
the initial condition $c$ or in the evaluation of $f(u)$
which happens all the time when we implement numerical algorithms.  
This leads to a perturbed solution $v(t) = u(t) + \epsilon(t)$, 
where $\epsilon(t)$ represents the deviation of the perturbed 
solution. Substituting $v$ into the ODE we find that 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d \epsilon}{d t} = f(u + \epsilon) - f(u) 
    = \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(u) \epsilon + \text{higher order terms}. 
\end{equation*}
Thus, roughly speaking, the perturbation $\epsilon$ solves 
a linear ODE of a similar form to the test ODE except that 
the coefficients $\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(u)$ 
depends on time technically. Thus, at least for 
small time intervals the perturbation remains 
controlled if 
$| 1 + \Delta t \frac{\partial f}{\partial u}(u)| \le 1$.

Let us now formalize our notion of stability a bit more.

\begin{definition}
    Consider the test ODE \eqref{eq:test_ode} with $\lambda 
    \in \C$ such that $\text{Re}(\lambda) < 0$. Suppose 
    $\{ u_n \}_{n=0}^\infty$ is a numerical solution 
    produced by an ODE solver. We say the solution is {\it absolutely 
    stable} if $|u_n| \le |u_{n-1}|$ for $n = 1, 2, \dots$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}
    For a given numerical method, the {\it region of absolute 
    stability} is the set of all $\lambda \Delta t \in \C$ 
    for which the numerical solution of the 
    test ODE \eqref{eq:test_ode} is absolutely stable.
\end{definition}

Thus, in the case of the forward Euler method this region of 
absolute stability is simply $\{ z \in \C \mid |1 + z| \le 1 \}$
while for backward Euler it is $\{ z \in \C \mid |1 - z| \ge 1 \}$.

\begin{definition}
    If the region of absolute stability of a numerical method 
    contains the entire left half of the complex plane, 
    we say that the method is {\it A-stable}.
\end{definition}

So forward Euler is {\it not} A-stable while backward 
Euler is indeed {\it A-stable}.

\section{Stability of backward Euler}
We are now finally ready to make sense of the difference 
we saw between the forward and backward Euler methods.
Apply backwards Euler to the test ODE to obtain the sequence 
\begin{equation*}
    u_n = u_{n-1} + \Delta t \lambda u_n.
\end{equation*}
Solving for $u_n$ we simply obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    u_n = (1 - \Delta t \lambda)^{-1} u_{n-1}.
\end{equation*}
In other words, 
\begin{equation*}
    |u_n| = |(1 - \Delta t \lambda)^{-1}| |u_{n-1}|.
\end{equation*}
Since $|(1 - \Delta t \lambda)^{-1}| \le 1$,
we see that the backward Euler method is absolutely stable 
for {\it any} choice of $\Delta t >0$ whenever 
$\text{Re}(\lambda) < 0$. In fact, the region of absolute 
stability for backward Euler is the entire 
complex plane minus the disk of radius 1 centered at 1
as shown in \Cref{fig:euler-stability-region}. This has 
profound practical implications: we are allowed to take as large
a step as we want without worrying about instabilities. 

\begin{quote}
    Be careful, stability does not mean accuracy. Using large time 
    steps still results in larger errors. The stability 
    only ensures that perturbations do not grow uncontrollably.
\end{quote}

\chapter{ODEs, PDEs, and the need for numerical methods
and analysis}
\label{ch:Lec1-intro}

In this course we focus on solution methods for some ordinary differential 
equations (ODEs) and partial differential equations (PDEs). Recall that 
an ODE involves functions of a single variable and their derivatives, while a PDE involves functions of multiple variables and their partial derivatives. A typical first 
order ODE is of the form 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:first_order_ode}
    \frac{du}{dt} = f(t,u),
\end{equation}
where $u:[a,b] \mapsto \R$ is a function of the single variable $t$ and $f:[a,b] \times \R \mapsto \R$ is a given function, often called the right-hand side of the ODE or a forcing term in the physical context. Higher order ODEs often take the form 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:high_order_ode}
    \frac{d^n u}{dt^n} = f(t,u,\frac{du}{dt},\frac{d^2 u}{dt^2},\dots,\frac{d^{n-1} u}{dt^{n-1}})
\end{equation}
where now $u:[a,b] \mapsto \R$ is a function of the single variable $t$ and $f:[a,b] \times \R^n \mapsto \R$ is  given. We say that \eqref{eq:high_order_ode} is an $n$-th order ODE.

ODEs are incredibly useful in modeling a wide range of physical, biological, and engineering systems, where the evolution of a quantity over time can be described by its rate of change. This is why we typically think of $u$ as a function of time $t$. 

\begin{example}[Harmonic Oscillator]\label{ex:harmonic_oscillator}
    The harmonic oscillator is described by the second-order ODE
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:mass_spring}
        \frac{d^2 u}{dt^2} + \omega^2 u = 0,
    \end{equation}
    where $u(t)$ represents the displacement from equilibrium and $\omega$ is the angular frequency. 
    This equation models the motion of a simple harmonic oscillator, such as a mass on a spring, where the restoring force is proportional to
    the displacement from equilibrium; see, e.g., 
    \cite[Ch.~5]{taylor2005classical} for a classical treatment. 
    In the case of the mass-spring system, $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$,
    where $k$ is the spring constant and $m$ is the mass, 
    see \Cref{fig:mass_spring}.

    Thinking purely in terms of the physics, the state of this 
    system should depend on its condition at some initial time, 
    for simplicty we take this time to be $t=0$. It is 
    natural for us to specify the initial conditions (IC) of the 
    system, which is typically the initial location/displacement
    as well as the velocity of the mass. Hence, we can supplement 
    \eqref{fig:mass_spring} with the ICs: 
    \begin{equation*}
        u(0) = u_0, \quad \frac{du}{dt}(0) = v_0.
    \end{equation*}
    With these conditions we can solve the problem analytically to 
    obtain the solution 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:harmonic_oscillator_solution}
        u(t) = u_0 \cos(\omega t) + \frac{v_0}{\omega} \sin(\omega t), \qquad 
        \forall t \ge 0.
    \end{equation}
    Plugging this solution back into the ODE verifies that 
    it satisfies it along with the ICs.

\end{example}

ODE models can also be extended to describe the evolution of 
various qunatities that are dependent on each other, often 
thought of as the evolution of a system of interacting components.
In this case we replace $u(t)$ as a scalar function with 
a vector-valued function $\ub: [a, b] \mapsto \R^d$, 
where $\ub(t) = (u_1(t), \dots, u_d(t))$ with $u_i(t)$ representing the $i$-th component of the system at time $t$. Then the evolution of the state of the 
system can be described by a system of ODEs of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:system_of_odes}
    \frac{d^n\ub}{dt^n} = \fb(t, \ub, \frac{d\ub}{dt}, \frac{d^2\ub}{dt^2}, \dots, \frac{d^{n-1}\ub}{dt^{n-1}}),
\end{equation}
where $\fb: [a, b] \times (\R^d)^n \mapsto \R^d$ is now the 
right hand side function. 

\begin{example}[Compartmental Disease Model]\label{ex:compartmental_disease_model}
    Let us consider a simple compartmental model for 
    the spread of disease in a population. In this model,
    the population is divided into different compartments based on their disease status, such as susceptible ($S$), infected ($I$), and recovered ($R$) individuals. The model tracks the number of individuals in each compartment over time.
    To make the notation consistent with \eqref{eq:system_of_odes}, 
    we define the state vector as $\ub = (S, I, R)$ and $n=1$. 

    The dynamics of the compartments can be described by the following system of ODEs:
    \begin{equation*}
            \frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta S I, \quad 
            \frac{dI}{dt} = \beta S I - \gamma I, \quad
            \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I.
    \end{equation*}
    where $\beta$ is the infection rate with $\beta SI$ denoting 
    the rate at which susceptible individuals become infected while 
    $\gamma$ is the recovery rate. This model is commonly referred to as the SIR model and is widely used in epidemiology to study the spread of infectious diseases, originally introduced in \cite{kermack1927contribution}.
    Note that this is overly simple as the model assumes that recovered
    individuals gain permanent immunity and cannot be reinfected.

    Interestingly, different choices of $\beta, \gamma$ can lead to
    qualitatively different dynamics, such as the occurrence of
    epidemics or the eventual eradication of the disease. We
    show some examples of this behavior in 
    Figure~\ref{fig:sir_dynamics}, obtained by simulating the 
    model with techniques that we will learn about in this course.
    In this example we need to specify ICs for all three compratments. 
    The numerics shown in the figure assume $S(0) = 0.95$, $I(0) = 0.05$, 
    and $R(0) = 0$, i.e., a small population of infected with a 
    large susceptible population.

    \end{figure}
\end{example}

In the example above we have already encountered the main topic 
of interest to this course. Even though in \Cref{ex:harmonic_oscillator}
we had an analytic solution to plot, the solutions in 
\Cref{ex:compartmental_disease_model} are numerical, and 
obtained by running a numerical algorithm. These examples 
already show how easily we can run into a complexity wall where 
traditional analytical methods may fail to provide solutions, 
necessitating the use of numerical techniques. This becomes 
even more apparent when we consider PDEs. 

A typical second order PDE in space-time takes the form 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:second_order_pde}
    \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = 
    f(t, u, \partial u/\partial x, \partial^2 u/\partial x^2),
\end{equation}
where $t$ represents time, say on the interval $[0,T]$ and 
$x$ represents the spatial coordinate, say on the interval $[0,L]$, and $u(t,x)$ is the unknown function of both time and space. Notice that now 
the function $u: [0,T] \times [0,L] \to \R$  depends on both time and space.
which makes this fundamentally different from the ODE setup. 
PDEs are generally more complex than ODEs and their study is a 
major area of mathematics \cite{evans2010partial}. This is for 
good reason since PDEs describe some of the most 
fundamental phenomena in physics, engineering, and other sciences.

\begin{example}[Heat Equation]\label{ex:heat_equation}
    The heat equation describes the distribution of heat (or variation in temperature) in a given region over time. In one spatial dimension, it takes the form
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \alpha \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2},
    \end{equation}
    where $u(t,x)$ represents the temperature at time $t$ and position $x$, and $\alpha > 0$ is the thermal diffusivity constant. This PDE is typically accompanied by initial and boundary conditions to fully specify the problem. Here we 
    will consider zero Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e.,
    \begin{equation}
        u(t,0) = u(t,L) = 0, \quad t \ge 0,
    \end{equation}
    and an initial condition
    \begin{equation}
        u(0,x) = u_0(x), \quad x \in [0,L].
    \end{equation}
    We show a numerical solution of this problem in \Cref{fig:heat_equation}
    where we compare a bad (unstable) numerical solution 
    with a good (stable) one. We clearly see that the left plot 
    is not physical, we anticipate heat would spread smoothly 
    over time rather than exhibiting erratic oscillations. 
    This is a numerical artifact, one that we will understand 
    deeply in this course.

        \label{fig:heat_equation}
    \end{figure}

\end{example}

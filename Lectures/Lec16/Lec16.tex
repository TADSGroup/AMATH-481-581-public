\chapter{Finite Difference Methods}

Motivated by the example from last lecture we now outline 
the general framework of Finite Difference 
Methods (FDM) for solving BVPs. 

\section{General Structure of a Finite Difference Method}

The family of FDMs for BVPs have a common three step 
structure: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Step 1: Pick a discretization grid or mesh, i.e., 
    a set of points $\{ t_0, \dots, t_N \} \subset [a, b]$
    \item Step 2: Discretize the differential equation using 
    difference formulae
    \item Step 3: Formulate and assemble a linear or nonlinear 
    system of algebraic equations and solve it  
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}\label{ex:l16:poisson}
    We already performed these steps for our toy BVP 
    \begin{equation*}
        u''(t) = f(t), \quad u(0) = c_0, \quad u(1) = c_1.
    \end{equation*} 
    Let us recall them for context. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Step 1: we may choose a uniform grid on 
        the interval $[0,1]$. So we set  
        $t_n = n \Delta t$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N$ 
        and $\Delta t = 1/N$. 
        
        \item Step 2: Since we impose Dirichlet B.C. there is 
        no approximation to do there. We only need to 
        discretize $u''$. Let $u_0, \dots, u_N$ denote 
        the numerical solutions s.t. $u_n \approx u(t_n)$. 
        Then we write 
        \begin{equation}\label{eq:l16-poisson}
            u''(t_n) \approx \frac{u_{n-1} - 2 u_n + u_{n+1}}{\Delta t^2}.
        \end{equation} 

        \item Step 3: Substituting the approx difference formula in
        the BVP and combining with the B.C. gives the system of 
        equations 
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{aligned}
                u_0  & = c_0 \\ 
                \frac{1}{\Delta t^2} ( u_{n-1} - 2 u_n + u_{n+1})  &= f_n, \qquad n =1, \dots, N-1 \\ 
                u_N & = c_1.
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation*}
        Now rewrite this system as a matrix equation 
        \begin{equation*}
            A \ub = \fb,
        \end{equation*}
        and compute $\ub = A^{-1} \fb$.
    \end{itemize}
    We show an example of solutions obtained through this framework 
    in \Cref{fig:l16:poisson-example}. We used homogeneous 
    Dirichlet conditions here with a smooth $f$ 
    in which case the problem has an analytic solution. This 
    allows us to investigate the convergence properties of 
    the method, in particular, plot the error 
    $e_N = \max_n |u_n - u(t_n)|$ as a function of the 
    number of grid points $N$. We see that $e_N =
     \mathcal{O}(N^{-2}) = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$. 
     This is not surprising since the centered difference formula 
     we used to approximate $u''$ is indeed second order accurate, 
     i.e., 
     \begin{equation*}
        \frac{1}{\Delta t^2} (u_{n-1} - 2 u_n + u_{n+1})
        = u''(t) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2).
     \end{equation*}

    \begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width = 0.48 \textwidth]{./Lectures/Lec16/fd_toy_bvp.png} 
        \includegraphics[width = 0.48 \textwidth]{./Lectures/Lec16/fd_toy_bvp_convergence.png} 
        \caption{An example solution of \eqref{eq:l16-poisson} 
        using FDM with $f(t) = - \exp( - \frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} | t - 1/2|^2)$ 
        taken to be a Gaussian bump with $\sigma = 0.1$. The left figure
        shows the numerical solutions for different choices of $N$ 
        while the right plot shows the error between the numerical 
        and analytic solution forthis problem. Notice that the error 
        decays quadratically in the number of grid points.}
        \label{fig:l16:poisson-example}
    \end{figure}
\end{example}

The choice of the difference formula used to 
approximate the derivative $u''$ facilitates 
different FDMs. For example, let us now consider 
a more general form of our second order ODE
but now with an additional first order derivative. 

\begin{example}
    Consider the BVP 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:l16:adv-diff}
        - u''(t) + 2 u'(t) = f(t), \qquad u(0) = u(1) = 0.
    \end{equation}
    For $f$ we will use 
    $f(t) = \exp( - \frac{1}{2 \sigma^2} | t - 1/2|^2) $ 
    with $\sigma = 0.1$. Let us proceed through the steps. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Step 1: As before we simply take $t_n = n \Delta t$ with 
        $\Delta t = 1/N$. 
        \item Step 2: We use the same centered difference 
        formulat for $u''$ but we use forward Euler for the $u'$ term, i.e., 
        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{aligned}
                -\frac{1}{\Delta t^2} (u_{n-1} - 2u_n + u_{n+1} )
                +  2 \cdot \frac{1}{\Delta t} (u_{n+1} - u_n) = f_n.
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation*}
        \item Step 3: Let $A$ denote the centered difference matrix from before. 
        Define the new matrix
        \begin{equation*}
            B = \frac{1}{\Delta t} \cdot 
            \begin{bmatrix}
                0 & 0 & 0 &  &\cdots  & 0 \\
                0 & -1 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
                0  &  0 & \ddots & \ddots & \cdots & 0 \\ 
                0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & -1 & 1  \\ 
                0 & \cdots & & 0 & 0 & 0
            \end{bmatrix}
        \end{equation*}
        that corresponds to the additional $u'$ term. 
        Then define $D = -A + 0.1 B$ and solve the system 
        \begin{equation*}
            D \ub = \fb.
        \end{equation*}
        We present similar numerical results to the 
        previous example in \Cref{fig:l16:adv-diff}. 
        Notice that this time the rate of 
        convergence is slow, in fact, it is only 
        $\mathcal{O}(N) = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t)$, 
        and so the method we designed is first order convergent. 
        As we will see later, this is due to the fact that 
        we used a first order approximation to $u'$ which 
        limits the convergence rate of our algorithm.
        \begin{figure}
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=0.48 \textwidth]{./Lectures/Lec16/fd_advection_bvp.png}
            \includegraphics[width=0.48 \textwidth]{./Lectures/Lec16/fd_advection_bvp_convergence.png}    
            \caption{Numerical solution of the BVP \eqref{eq:l16:adv-diff} 
            with homogeneous Dirichlet B.C. mirroring the 
            results from \Cref{ex:l16:poisson}. Notice that the rate of 
            convergence is now 1 rather than 2.}
            \label{fig:l16:adv-diff}       
        \end{figure}
    \end{itemize}
\end{example}

\section{On error analysis for BVP solvers}

Let us now take a closer look at the error analysis for BVP solvers and try 
to justify why we see $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$ convergence for 
\Cref{ex:l16:poisson}. Define the truncation error 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l16:local-truncation}
    \tau_n = \frac{u(t_{n-1}) - 2 u(t_n) + u(t_{n+1})}{\Delta t^2} - f(t_n),
\end{equation}
that is, the residual of the discretized ODE after plugging in the 
true solution. Also define the global error 
\begin{equation*}
    e_n = u(t_n) - u_n,
\end{equation*}
both of these are the quantities that we have worked with when we analyzed 
the error of IVP solvers. Now also note that since $u_n$ is the solution 
to the discrete problem then 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{u_{n-1} - 2 u_n + u_{n+1}}{\Delta t^2} - f(t_n) = 0.
\end{equation*}
Hence, subtracting the above expression from \eqref{eq:l16:local-truncation}
we obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    \tau_n = \frac{ e_{n-1} - 2 e_n + e_{n+1}}{\Delta t ^2}, 
\end{equation*}
which holds for $n=1, \dots, N-1$. On the boundaries we have $u_0 = u(t_0)
= c_0$ and $u_N = u(1) = c_1$ so the error is zero 
\begin{equation*}
    e_0 = e_N = 0.
\end{equation*}
Write $\eb = (e_1, \dots, e_{N-1})$ and $\pmb{\tau} = (\tau_1, \dots, \tau_{N-1})$. 
Then we have that 
\begin{equation*}
    A \eb = \Delta t^2 \pmb{\tau} \Rightarrow \eb = \Delta t^2 A^{-1} \pmb{\tau}, 
\end{equation*}
where 
\begin{equation*}
    A = \begin{bmatrix}
        -2 & 1 & 0 & & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        1 &  -2 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
        0 & 1 & -2 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ 
         &  & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & \\ 
         0 & \cdots & 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 \\ 
         0 & \cdots &   & 0 & 1 & -2
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}
Thus, we have that 
\begin{equation*}
    \| \eb \|_\infty \le \Delta t^2 \| A^{-1} \|_\infty \| \pmb{\tau}\|_\infty,
\end{equation*}
where we recall that for an arbitrary vector $\xb$ the $\infty$-norm 
is defined as 
\begin{equation*}
    \| \xb\|_{\infty} = \max_{j} |x_j|
\end{equation*}
and furthermore, 
\begin{equation*}
    \| A \|_{\infty} = \max_{\xb} \frac{\| A \xb \|_{\infty}}{\| \xb \|_{\infty}}
    = \max_{i} \sum_{j} |a_{ij}|.
\end{equation*}
It can be shown that $\| A^{-1} \|_\infty \le \frac{N^2}{4}$ 
and since $\Delta t = 1/N$ then we have the bound, 
\begin{equation*}
    \| \eb \|_\infty \le 
     \frac{1}{N^2}\frac{N^2}{4} \| \pmb \tau \|_\infty
     = \frac{1}{4} \| \pmb \tau \|_\infty.
\end{equation*}
At the same time, we already checked that 
$|\tau_n| = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$ which implies 
$\| \pmb \tau \|_\infty = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$ 
and so $\| \eb \|_\infty = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)$.

\chapter{Solving nonlinear systems and an introduction 
to convergence}
\label{lec:accuracy}

In this lecture we extend the Newton method to systems of 
ODEs to obtain a complete algorithm for implementing 
backward Euler for nonlinear systems of ODEs. 
Afterwards we briefly introduce the notion of a 
convergent method which sets us up for upcoming 
theoretical concepts.


\section{Solving systems of ODEs}

Finally we generalize our methods to systems of ODEs which also allows us to 
solve higher order ODEs. Given the problem 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d}{dt} \ub = \fb(t, \ub), \qquad \ub(0) = \cb,
\end{equation*}
the forward Euler method is simply of the form 
\begin{equation*}
    \ub_{n+1} = \ub_n + \Delta t \, \fb(t_n, \ub_n),
\end{equation*}
essentially follows exactly the scalar case. The backward Euler method 
also has the familiar form 
\begin{equation*}
    \ub_{n+1} = \ub_n + \Delta t \, \fb(t_{n+1}, \ub_{n+1}).
\end{equation*} 
However, we now realize that each iteration of backward Euler requires the solution 
of a nonlinear system. For this we simply use the extension of Newton's method to systems: 
given the nonlinear equation 
\begin{equation*}
    \gb(\vb) = 0, 
\end{equation*}
the Newton iteration takes the form 
\begin{equation*}
    \vb_{k+1} = \vb_{k} - \left( D \gb(\vb_{k}) \right)^{-1} \gb(\vb_{k}),
\end{equation*}
where $D \gb$ denotes the Jacobian matrix of $\gb$ as in \Cref{def:jacobian}.
For backward Euler the Newton sub-iterations take the particular form 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    \vb_{k+1} & = \vb_{k} - \left( D_{\ub} \left[ \vb_{k} - \ub_n - \Delta t \, \fb(t_{n+1}, \vb_{k}) \right] \right)^{-1} \left[ \vb_{k} - \ub_n - \Delta t \, \fb(t_{n+1}, \vb_{k}) \right] \\
    & = \vb_{k} - \left( I - \Delta t \, D_{\ub} \fb(t_{n+1}, \vb_{k}) \right)^{-1} \left[ \vb_{k} - \ub_n - \Delta t \, \fb(t_{n+1}, \vb_{k}) \right].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Note that we now need to have access to $D\fb$ and invert a matrix at 
each iteration of Newton's method. $D \fb$ can be approximated in many 
ways although nowadays automatic differentiation tools are often used to 
compute it efficiently. The inversion of the matrix can also be done 
using various techniques from linear algebra that we will not discuss 
for now. Let us summarize the algorithm for completeness: 

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Backward Euler with Newton Subiterations for Systems}\label{alg:backward_euler_newton_systems}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Given $\cb$, $\fb$, $D_{\ub}\fb$, $\Delta t$, and tolerance $\text{tol} > 0$
\State Set $\ub_0 = \cb$ 
\For{$n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, T/\Delta t - 1$}
\State Initialize $\vb_0 = \ub_n$
\State Set $k = 0$
\Repeat
    \State Set $\vb_{k+1} = \vb_k - \left( I - \Delta t \, D_{\ub} \fb(t_{n+1}, \vb_k) \right)^{-1} \left[ \vb_k - \ub_n - \Delta t \, \fb(t_{n+1}, \vb_k) \right]$
    \State Increment $k \gets k+1$
\Until{$|\vb_{k+1} - \vb_k| < \text{tol}$}
\State Set $\ub_{n+1} = \vb_{k+1}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{A motivating example: predator-prey model}
Let us consider the Lotka-Volterra predator-prey model that models the interaction between predators and prey. The system of ODEs is given by
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{du_1}{dt} & = \alpha u_1 - \beta u_1 u_2, \\
    \frac{du_2}{dt} & = \delta u_1 u_2 - \gamma u_2,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
where $u_1(t)$ represents the prey population, $u_2(t)$ represents the 
predator population, and $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, $\delta$ are 
positive constants. Here the right hand side takes the form 
\begin{equation*}
    \fb(t, \ub) = \begin{bmatrix}
    \alpha u_1 - \beta u_1 u_2 \\
    \delta u_1 u_2 - \gamma u_2
    \end{bmatrix}, 
    \qquad D_{\ub} \fb(t, \ub) = \begin{bmatrix}
    \alpha - \beta u_2 & -\beta u_1 \\
    \delta u_2 & \delta u_1 - \gamma
    \end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation*}

\section{Convergence}

Convergence of a numerical method often refers to the property that 
as the {\it resolution} or {\it capacity} of the model increases then 
the numerical solution converges to the true solution of the problem. 
In case of ODEs, this often takes the following form. 

\begin{definition}[Convergence of an ODE solver]
    Consider an ODE with a ground truth solution $u: [0, T] \mapsto \R$ and 
    an ODE solver that, for an integer $N$, produces a numerical solution 
    $u_n \approx u(t_n)$ at time $t_n = n \Delta t$ for $n =0, 1, \dots, N$
    and with $\Delta t = T /N$. Then we say that the method is  
    {\it convergent} if
    \begin{equation}
        \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \max_{0 \le n \le N} |u(t_n) - u_n| = 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{definition}

Notice that one can always adjust this definition for different purposes. For 
example if we care only about the solution at the final time $T$, we could ask 
for $\lim_{\Delta t \to 0} |u(T) - u_N| = 0$. Also note that the 
notion of convergence somehow depends on the ground truth solution $u$ as well
and we need, at the very least, that solution to be well-defined.

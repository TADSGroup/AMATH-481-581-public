\chapter{Stability for systems plus stiffness}

This lecture covers two important additional topics, one 
is the extension of absolute stability to systems of ODEs 
and second is the concept of stiffness that 
sits at the intersection of stability and accuracy. 

\section{Stability for systems}

We can naturally extend our notion of absolute stability 
to systems of ODEs by considering the test problem 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d\ub}{dt} = A \ub,
    \quad \ub(0) = \cb,
\end{equation*}
where we assume that $A$ is a diagonalizable matrix. Under 
this assumption we can factorize $A$ as 
$A = Q \Lambda Q^{-1},$ where $Q \in \C^{d \times d}$ is 
the matrix of eigenvectors of $A$ and 
$\Lambda \in \C^{d \times d}$ is the diagonal matrix 
of its eigenvalues. Multiplying both sides of the ODE 
by $Q^{-1}$ we obtain the alternative form 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d \zb}{dt} = \Lambda \zb,
    \quad \zb(0) = Q^{-1} \cb,
\end{equation*}
where $\zb(t) = Q^{-1} \ub(t)$ is the transformed variable. Since 
$\lambda$ is diagona, this system is decoupleda and is equivalent 
to solving $d$ independent scalar ODEs of the form 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d z_i}{dt} = \lambda_i z_i,
    \quad z_i(0) = (Q^{-1} \cb)_i.
\end{equation*}
Thus our previous ideas for absolute stability for scalar ODEs 
can be directly applied to each of the decoupled scalar ODEs 
in the transformed system. 

For example, for the forward Euler 
method, we obtain stability if each of the above 
scalar ODEs satisfy the condition $|1 + \Delta t \lambda_i| < 1$ 
for all $i = 1, \dots, d$. When $\lambda_i$ is real and negative then 
we obtain the bound 
\begin{equation*}
    0 < \Delta t < \frac{2}{|\lambda_i|}.
\end{equation*}
So stability is dictated by the absolute largest eigenvalue of $A$.
Similarly, for backward Euler we obtain the 
stability condition $|1 - \Delta t \lambda_i| < 1$ 
for all $i = 1, \dots, d$. So backward Euler remains 
$A$-stable for systems of ODEs as well.


\section{Stiffness}
Stiffness is a concept that arises in the context of numerical 
solution of ODEs and PDEs that reveals a tension between 
stability and accuracy. It is somewhat qualitative and 
so it is best described in the context of an example. 

\begin{example}
Consider the ODE 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stiff-ODE-example}
    \frac{du}{dt} = - 100 ( u - \sin(t))
    \quad u(0) = 1,
\end{equation}
for $t \ge 0$. Notice that due to the coefficient $-100$ the 
solution to this ODE will decay towards zero very rapidly since 
$\sin(t)$ is small when $t$ is near zero. However, the sinusoid 
will eventually dominate the bevaior of the solution when $t$ 
becomes larger, say closer to $\pi /2$. \Cref{fig:stiff-ODE-example}
 shows a plot of the solution to this problem computed using 
the forward and backward Euler methods.

We see that backward Euler remains stable, as expected, 
even for larget values of $\Delta t$. On the other hand, 
forward Euler becomes unstable roughly when  we pick 
$\Delta t > \frac{2}{100} = 0.02$ which is the dominant 
eigenvalue of the system in the initial phase.

\end{example}


The above example illustrates the typical image we have in mind 
when we think of a stiff ODE or problem. That is, a problem 
where a severe restriction is imposed on explicit time-stepping 
methods such as forward Euler but only for a brief period of 
time. Once this period is cleared then the solution can be 
approximated well with much larger time steps. Since implicit 
methods such as backward Euler are unconditionally stable, 
they can handle the stiff initial phase without
requiring excessively small time steps and so they are often 
recommended for stiff problems. 

Another manifestation of stiffness is when dealing with systems 
of ODEs. Here we may have some coordinates that have very 
large eigenvalues, i.e., fast dynamics, evolving alongside 
other coordinates that have much smaller eigenvalues, i.e., 
slow dynamics. Then the fast coordinates will impose a 
severe time restriction on explicit methods while 
the slow coordinates can be advanced with much larger time steps, highlighting the stiffness of the system. We demonstrate an example of this below. 

\begin{example}
    Consider the linear system 
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{d u_1}{dt} = - u_1, 
      \quad \frac{d u_2}{dt} = - \lambda ( u_2 - u_1),
      \quad (u_1, u_2)(0) = c_1, c_2.
    \end{equation*}
    Notice that the right hand side can 
    be written in matrix form 
    as 
    \begin{equation*}
         A \ub = \begin{pmatrix}
            -1 & 0 \\
            \lambda & -\lambda
         \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}
            u_1 \\
            u_2
         \end{pmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    and the eigenvalues of the matrix $A$ are $-1$ and $-\lambda$. So 
    if $\lambda \gg 1$, then the system exhibits stiffness 
    because the second equation has a much faster timescale 
    compared to the first one. Indeed, we can solve this 
    system analytically: 
    \begin{equation*}
        u_1(t) = c_1 e^{-t}, \quad 
        u_2(t) = \frac{\lambda c_1}{\lambda - 1} e^{-t} + 
        \left( c_2 - \frac{\lambda c_1}{\lambda -1} \right) e^{-\lambda t}.
    \end{equation*}
    We can see that if $\lambda \gg 1$ then $u_2$ gets pulled 
    towards $u_1$ very quickly while $u_1$ itself 
    evolves slowly in time. 
\end{example}

In light of the above example, stiffness of a linear system of ODEs 
can be characterized in terms of the ratio of 
the largest to the smallest (in magnitude) eigenvalue of the system matrix. This ratio is often referred to as the stiffness ratio. 
A large stiffness ratio indicates that the system has widely varying timescales, which can lead to stiffness in numerical simulations. For nonlinear 
ODEs we consider the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix 
of the system in the same manner. But keep in mind that the Jacobian 
will change as the solution evolves in this case.

\begin{quote}
    The practical message here is that if a problem  is 
    stiff, it is advisable to pay the additional computational 
    cost of  using an implicit method, such as backward Euler, or 
    other specialized solvers that can handle stiffness. 
\end{quote}

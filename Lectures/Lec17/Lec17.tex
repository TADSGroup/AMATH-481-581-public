\chapter{Solving a Variable Coefficient Second 
Order BVP}

In this lecture we will derive a second order FDM method 
for solving a variable coefficients BVP to show various 
important considerations and ideas that are 
involved in these problems beyond the toy 
examples we have seen so far. Consider 
the BVP 
\begin{equation*}
    - \frac{d}{dt} \left( 
       a(t) \frac{d u}{dt} (t)
    \right) 
    + b(t) \frac{d u}{dt} (t)
    + c(t) u (t) = f(t), \qquad t \in (0,1) 
\end{equation*}
where $a, b, c, f$ are given functions,
subject to the Robin boundary conditions 
\begin{equation*}
    a(0)\frac{du}{dt}(0) = \alpha_0 u(0) + \beta_0,
    \qquad 
    a(1)\frac{du}{dt}(1) = \alpha_1 u(1) + \beta_1,
\end{equation*}
with given coefficients $\alpha_0, \alpha_1, 
\beta_0, \beta_1$. 
This problem mostly contains every feature we 
need to deal with when solving 
a linear second order BVP. 

We will proceed in our usual three step approach: 

\section*{Step 1: A uniform grid}
We proceed with a uniform grid on the interval 
$[0,1]$ where $t_j = j \Delta t$ for $j=0, \cdots, N$ 
and $\Delta t = 1/N$. Later we will augment 
this grid with a pair of so-called ghost points 
that help us impose gradient conditions on 
the boundary points. To this end, we also 
consider the points $t_{-1} = - \Delta t$ and $t_{N+1} = 1+ \Delta t$; 
see \Cref{fig:uniform-grid-plus-ghost-points}.

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{./Lectures/Lec17/grid_ghost_points.pdf}
    \caption{The uniform grid $t_j = j \Delta t$, $j = 0, \dots, N$, on $[0,1]$.
    The boundary points $t_0 = 0$ and $t_N = 1$ are shown as black dots
    and the ghost points $t_{-1}$ and $t_{N+1}$ as red crosses.}
    \label{fig:uniform-grid-plus-ghost-points}
\end{figure}

\section*{Step 2: Discretize the BVP}
We will choose a discretization of the 
BVP by replacing all differential operators
with second order accurate difference formulae. 

Let us start with the $0$-order term, which 
is effectively  trivial to discretize
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l17:diag-op}
    c(t_j)u(t_j) 
    \approx c_j u_j
\end{equation}
where we simply wrote $c_j = c(t_j)$.

Next consider the first order term 
\begin{equation*}
    b \frac{d u}{dt},
\end{equation*}
to discretize this term we only need to approximate
$u'$ and for this we choose a centered difference formula 
which is second order accurate 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l17:adv-op}
    b(t_j) \frac{d u}{dt}(t_j)
    = b(t_j) \frac{u(t_{j+1}) - u(t_{j-1})}
    {2 \Delta t} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2)
    \approx b_j \frac{u_{j+1} - u_{j-1}}{2 \Delta t},
\end{equation}
where again we wrote $b_j = b(t_j)$.

Next consider the second order term 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d}{d t} \left( a \frac{d u}{dt} \right).
\end{equation*}
To discretize this term we need to take 
some care since we prefer to 
avoid differentiating the coefficient 
$a$. 
\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{./Lectures/Lec17/diffusion_stencil.pdf}
    \caption{Zoomed in view of the grid around $t_j$ showing the 
    two applications of the centered difference formula used to 
    discretize the diffusion term. The coefficient $a$ is sampled 
    on the staggered grid $t_{j\pm 1/2}$ (hollow blue squares). 
    In the first level (blue) the grid values $u_{j-1}, u_j, u_{j+1}$
    (black dots) are combined to approximate $a u'$ at the midpoints. 
    In the second level (red) these midpoint values are combined 
    to approximate $(a u')'$ at $t_j$.}
    \label{fig:diffusion-centered-stencile}
\end{figure}

We start by using centered 
difference formulae to estimate the value of 
$u'$ at the midpoints $t_{j+1/2} = t_j + \Delta t/2$ 
and $t_{j-1/2} = t_j - \Delta t/2$
(see \Cref{fig:diffusion-centered-stencile}), i.e., 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{d u}{dt}(t_{j+1/2})
    & = \frac{u(t_{j+1}) - u(t_j)}{\Delta t}
    - \frac{\Delta t^2}{24} u'''(t_{j+1/2})
    + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4) \\
    \frac{d u}{dt}(t_{j-1/2})
    & = \frac{u(t_{j}) - u(t_{j-1})}{\Delta t}
    - \frac{\Delta t^2}{24} u'''(t_{j-1/2})
    + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4)        
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Note that the remainder terms are $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4)$
rather than $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^3)$ since the centered
difference formula only produces even powers of $\Delta t$
in its error expansion.
Now multiply these expressions by 
$ a_{j+1/2} = a(t_{j+1/2}) $ and 
$ a_{j-1/2}= a(t_{j-1/2})$ in that 
order  so that we have 
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
    \left( a \frac{d u}{dt} \right)(t_{j+1/2})
& = a_{j+1/2}
 \left( \frac{u(t_{j+1}) - u(t_j)}{\Delta t} \right)  
 - \frac{\Delta t^2}{24} a_{j+1/2}  u'''(t_{j+1/2}) 
+ \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4)\\ 
   \left( a \frac{d u}{dt} \right)(t_{j-1/2}) 
& = a_{j-1/2}
 \left( \frac{u(t_{j}) - u(t_{j-1})}{\Delta t} \right)  
 - \frac{\Delta t^2}{24} a_{j-1/2}  u'''(t_{j-1/2}) 
  + 
 \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Then applying the centered difference formula once more,
this time centered at $t_j$ with step $\Delta t/2$, 
to the function $F = a u'$ we have
\begin{equation*}
    F'(t_j) = \frac{F(t_{j+1/2}) - F(t_{j-1/2})}{\Delta t}
    - \frac{\Delta t^2}{24} F'''(t_j) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4).
\end{equation*}
Substituting the expressions for $F(t_{j\pm 1/2})$ from above
requires some care since the error terms
get divided by $\Delta t$ and one may worry that we lose
an order of accuracy. This does not happen: 
the $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4)$ remainders become $\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^3)$,
while the leading error terms appear as a difference, 
\begin{equation*}
    - \frac{\Delta t^2}{24} 
    \frac{a_{j+1/2} u'''(t_{j+1/2}) - a_{j-1/2} u'''(t_{j-1/2})}{\Delta t}
    = - \frac{\Delta t^2}{24} (a u''')'(t_j) + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^4)
    = \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2),
\end{equation*}
where we used the centered difference formula once again, now 
applied to the function $a u'''$. Putting everything together
we obtain
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    \frac{d}{dt} \left( a \frac{du}{dt}  \right)(t_j)
    &= \frac{1}{\Delta t}
    \left[ 
    a(t_{j+1/2})    
    \left( \frac{u(t_{j+1}) - u(t_j)}{\Delta t} \right)
    - 
    a(t_{j-1/2})    
    \left( \frac{u(t_{j}) - u(t_{j-1})}
    {\Delta t} \right)
    \right] 
    + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2) \\
     &\approx \frac{1}{\Delta t^2} 
     \left[ a_{j+1/2} (u_{j+1} - u_j) 
     - a_{j-1/2} ( u_j - u_{j-1})  \right]
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
In summary, we have the second order approximation 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:l17:diffusion-op}
    \frac{d}{dt} \left( a \frac{du}{dt} \right) (t_j)
\approx    \frac{1}{\Delta t^2} 
     \left[ a_{j+1/2} u_{j+1} 
     - (a_{j+1/2} + a_{j-1/2}) u_j 
        + a_{j-1/2} u_{j-1}
     \right].
\end{equation}
Notice that if $a = 1$ then this formula reduces
to the usual centered difference approximation 
to $u''$. 

Now we consider the boundary conditions. Since Robin 
conditions involve $u'$ at $t_0$ and $t_N$
then we need to approximate those values 
using second order formulae. This means we have 
two options: 

\begin{itemize}
    \item {\bf (Option 1: One-sided differences)}
    We simply use one-sided formulae to approximate $u'(t_0)$ and 
    $u'(t_N)$: 
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
        u'(t_0) & = \frac{ - 3 u(t_0) + 4 u(t_1) - u(t_2)}{2 \Delta t} 
        + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2), \\
        u'(t_N) & = \frac{3 u(t_N) - 4 u(t_{N-1}) + u(t_{N-2})}{2 \Delta t}
        + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2).
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
    Hence, we will use the approximations 
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
        u'(t_0) & = \frac{-3 u_0 + 4 u_1 - u_2}{2 \Delta t}, \\
        u'(t_N) & = \frac{3 u_N - 4 u_{N-1} + u_{N-2}}{2 \Delta t},
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
    which we can substitute into the Robin conditions 
    to get 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:l17:robin-one-sided}
        \begin{aligned}
            a_0 \frac{-3 u_0 + 4 u_1 - u_2}{2 \Delta t}
            - \alpha_0 u_0  & = \beta_0 \\
            a_N \frac{3 u_N - 4 u_{N-1} + u_{N-2}}{2 \Delta t}
            - \alpha_1 u_N & = \beta_1.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}


    \item {\bf (Option 2: Centered difference with 
    ghost points)} 
    Alternatively, we apply centered difference formulae 
    to estimate $u'$ at both end points 
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
            u'(0) & = \frac{u(t_1) - u(t_{-1})}{2 \Delta t} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2), \\
            u'(1) & = \frac{u(t_{N+1}) - u(t_{N-1})}{2 \Delta t} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta t^2).
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
    Hence, we can use these formulae to impose the 
    boundary conditions 
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:l17:robin-centered}
        \begin{aligned}
            a_0 \frac{u_1 - u_{-1}}{2 \Delta t}
            - \alpha_0 u_0  & = \beta_0 \\
            a_N \frac{u_{N+1} -  u_{N-1}}{2 \Delta t}
            - \alpha_1 u_N & = \beta_1.
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    Since we now have additional variables, i.e., $u_{-1}$ and 
    $u_{N+1}$ we also impose the discrete ODE at 
    $t_0$ and $t_N$ to close the system.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Step 3: Assemble matrices}
With our discretizations at hand, we now need to assemble 
the requisite matrices for our linear system to solve for 
the solution vector $\ub$. Let us begin term by term 
following our derivation of the difference formulae.

\begin{itemize}
    \item {\bf The $c(t)u(t)$ term:} This term is simply represented
    via a diagonal matrix, recall \eqref{eq:l17:diag-op}, 
    \begin{equation*}
        c(t_j) u(t_j) \approx (C \ub)_j, \qquad C = 
        \begin{bmatrix}
            0 & & & & & & \\ 
              & c_1 & &  & & &\\
              & & c_2 &  & & &\\
              & & &  c_3 & & &\\ 
              & & &   &  \ddots & & \\
              & & & & &    c_{N-1} & \\ 
              & & & & &    &  0
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation*} 
    Notice the first and last rows are zero since 
    those will be given by the boundary conditions. 
    \item {\bf The $b u'$ term:} This term is 
    represented, following \eqref{eq:l17:adv-op}, as 
    \begin{equation*}
        b(t_j) u'(t_j) \approx (B \ub)_j \qquad 
        B = \frac{1}{2 \Delta t} 
        \begin{bmatrix}
            0 & & & & & & \\ 
            -b_1 &  0     & b_1 & & & & \\ 
                 & - b_2  & 0   & b_2 & & & \\ 
                 &        & \ddots & \ddots & \ddots & & \\ 
                 &   &  & -b_{N-2}   & 0 &b_{N-2} & \\
                 &   &  &  & -b_{N-1}   & 0 &b_{N-1} \\
                 & & & & & & 0
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{equation*}
    \item {\bf The $(a u')'$ term:} For this term we simply 
    write \eqref{eq:l17:diffusion-op} in matrix form 
    by ignoring the boundary terms once more, 
    {\scriptsize
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
        &(a u')'(t_j) \approx (A \ub)_j,\\ 
        &A = \frac{1}{\Delta t^2} 
        \begin{bmatrix}
            0 & & & & & &  \\
            a_{1-1/2} & -(a_{1-1/2} + a_{1+1/2}) & a_{1 + 1/2} & &  \\
                      & a_{2 - 1/2} & -(a_{2 -1/2 } + a_{2 + 1/2}) & a_{2 + 1/2}&  \\
                      &       \ddots        & \ddots & \ddots &    \\
                      &  a_{N-2 - 1/2} & -(a_{N-2 - 1/2} + a_{N-2 + 1/2})  & a_{N-2 + 1/2} &  \\
                      &   & a_{N-1-1/2} & -(a_{N - 1-1/2} + a_{N-1 +1/2})  & a_{N-1+1/2}   \\
                                                                                & & & &   0
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
    }
    \item {\bf The boundary conditions:} Finally, using the 
    one sided formulae for the derivatives on the boundary
    we can approximate the boundary operators, reading off of
     \eqref{eq:l17:robin-one-sided}, 
     via the matrix $R$ defined below
    \begin{equation*}
        R = 
        \begin{bmatrix}
         \frac{-3 a_0}{2 \Delta t} - \alpha_0 & 
        \frac{2 a_0}{\Delta t} & -\frac{a_0}{2 \Delta t}  &  & \\ 
        & & & & \\ 
        & & \frac{a_N}{2 \Delta t} & -\frac{2 a_N}{\Delta t} & \frac{3 a_N}{2 \Delta t} - \alpha_1         
        \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation*}
    Notice that this matrix is zero everywhere except for the first and last rows. 
    Then $R \ub$ returns a vector which is only nonzero in its 
    first and last entries. 
\end{itemize}
Finally, write 
\begin{equation*}
    D = -A + B + C + R , \qquad \fb = (\beta_0, f_1, \dots, f_{N-1}, \beta_1). 
\end{equation*}
Then the numerical solution $\ub$ can be computed by solving the 
linear system 
\begin{equation*}
    D \ub = \fb.
\end{equation*}
As usual, this matrix is very sparse and so the system can be 
solved efficiently using sparse linear algebra or iterative schemes.

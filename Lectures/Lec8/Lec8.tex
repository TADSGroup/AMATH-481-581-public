\chapter{The fundamental theorem of Lax and introduction to stability}
\label{ch:fund-lax-intro-stability}

In the last lecture we showed that forward Euler is convergent of 
order one using direct calculations. Doing this is difficult for more 
complicated methods so it is often helpful to break the problem 
into smaller subproblems. This can be achieved using a 
fundamental theorem due to Peter Lax that allows us 
to combine order of consistency/accuracy (think $\tau_n$) 
with the notion of $0$-stability to obtain order of convergence.  


\section{Consistency + $0$-stability = convergence}
Let us begin by defining $0$-stability for a numerical scheme. 

\begin{definition}[$0$-stability]
    Consider a numerical ODE solver with a difference operator 
    $\mcl{N}_{\Delta t}$. We say the method is $0$-stable 
    if there exist constants $C, h_0 >0$ 
    independent of $\Delta t$ 
    such that
    for all $\Delta t \le h_0$ and 
     any two sequences $\{v_n\}_{n=1}^N$ and 
    $\{w_n\}_{n=1}^N$  
    we have the bound 
    \begin{equation*}
        |v_n - w_n| \le C \Big[ | v_0 - w_0| 
        + \max_{1 \le j \le n} \left| \mcl{N}_{\Delta t}[v_{j-1}] - 
        \mcl{N}_{\Delta t}[w_{j-1}] \right| \Big].
    \end{equation*}
\end{definition}
If we think of $v_n$ and $w_n$ as two
nearby numerical solutions 
to our ODE then $0$-stability says that for small enough $\Delta t$ 
the difference between the two numerical solutions 
remains bounded in terms of the initial conditions as well as 
the perturbations introduced by 
applying the difference operator $\mcl{N}_{\Delta t}$. The "0" in 
the $0$-stability terminology refers to the fact that we 
require $\Delta t$ to be sufficiently small, hence it is 
often considered to be a notion of stability in the
limit $\Delta t \to 0$.

With this definition at hand, we obtain the following fundamental 
theorem which is known as the Lax equivalence theorem due to 
Peter Lax. 

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:lax-equivalence}
    A numerical method that is consistent and $0$-stable 
    is also convergent. In fact, if the method is 
    consistent of order $p$ then it is also convergent 
    of order $p$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    Take the sequences $v_n = u(t_n)$ and $ w_n = u_n$ produced 
    by our numerical scheme. Then substituting in the 
    definition of $0$-stability, we obtain 
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{aligned}
        |e_n| & \le C \Big[ e_0 + \max_{1 \le j \le n} | \mcl{N}_{\Delta t}[u(t_{j-1})] - 
        \mcl{N}_{\Delta t}[u_{j-1}]| \Big] \\ 
        & \le C \max_{1 \le j \le n} | \tau_j | = \mcl{O}(\Delta t^p).
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
\end{proof}
The proof of this result is simple which implies that most of 
the work in showing the order of convergence of a method 
has to go into verifying its conditions. Namely, for any 
other method you have to first obtain a bound on the 
local truncation errors $\tau_n$ and then prove that the 
resulting difference operator $\mcl{N}_{\Delta t}$ is $0$-stable. 
For both of these steps we often rely heavily on Taylor expansions 
as well as additional smoothness assumptions as we 
did in the forward Euler example.

For the forward Euler method we have already verified first order 
consistency. Proving that the method is $0$-stable is left as an exercise 
in the homework sets and follows a somewhat simialr calculation to 
our proof from the previous lecture.

\section{Motivation for stability analysis}

Now that we have an idea of how the error analysis can be performed 
for a numerical ODE solver we can turn our attention to 
other important issues that affect the quality of our numerical 
solutions. In particular we will focus on the issue of stability 
\footnote{Note that despite the terminology this will turn out 
to be very different from $0$-stability.}. 

To motivate the idea let us consider the following simple ODE 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stiff-ODE}
    \frac{d u}{d t} = - 100 ( u - \sin(t)), \quad u(0)=1. 
\end{equation}
The numerical solution to this problem using both forward and backward 
Euler is presented in \Cref{fig:instability}. We see that 
when $\Delta t$ is large the forward and backward scheme exhibit very 
different behavior. Indeed forward Euler predicts large spurious 
oscillations that are not present in the backward Euler solution. 
The goal of stability analysis is to understand this phenomenon and 
come up with conditions or constraints that avoid it. 


\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48 \textwidth]{Lectures/Lec8/stiff_forward_euler}
    \includegraphics[width=0.48 \textwidth]{Lectures/Lec8/stiff_backward_euler}
    \caption{Nunmerical solution of \eqref{eq:stiff-ODE} using 
    forward Euler (left) and backward Euler (right). Notice the 
    extra oscillations that appear in the forward Euler solution that 
    appear to vanish for sufficiently small $\Delta t$. But these 
    oscillations are absent for backward Euler.}
    \label{fig:instability}
\end{figure}

\begin{quote}
    While accuracy and convergence analysis involve the study of 
    the asymptotic behavior of a method in the small $\Delta t$ limit, 
    stability analysis is concerned with the behavior of algorithms 
    for larger values of $\Delta t$. 
\end{quote}

Motivated by the above example, let us now consider an even simpler 
problem, i.e., our test ODE 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d u}{d t} = \lambda u, \quad u(0) = c
\end{equation*}
where we now assume $\lambda, c, u(t) \in \C$. Let us suppose that 
we make a small error in prescribing $c$
 and instead of $u$ we compute $u_\epsilon$ that 
solves 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d u_\epsilon}{d t} = \lambda u_\epsilon, \quad u_\epsilon(0) = c_\epsilon. 
\end{equation*}
Here $\epsilon$ is a parameter and we have in mind a setting where 
$u(t) - u_\epsilon(t)$ is small. Then the difference between 
the true solution and the perturbed solution satisfies the ODE 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d (u - u_\epsilon) }{d t} = \lambda (u - u_\epsilon), 
    \qquad (u - u_\epsilon)(0) = c - c_\epsilon.
\end{equation*}
Our hope is that for large $t$ the difference $u - u_\epsilon$ vanishes. 
This is clearly the case for the ODE provided that $\Re(\lambda) < 0$. 
Then we can ask whether a numerical scheme also satisfies this 
property? i.e., do small errors made along the way vanish after 
some time? or do they get amplified and corrupt the whole 
solution?

Applying forward Euler to the above ODE we obtain the sequence 
\begin{equation*}
    (u - u_\epsilon)_n = (u - u_\epsilon)_{n-1} 
    + \Delta t \lambda (u - u_\epsilon)_{n-1} 
    = ( 1 + \Delta t \lambda) ( u - u_\epsilon)_{n-1}, 
\end{equation*}
which in turn implies that 
\begin{equation*}
    (u - u_\epsilon)_n = (1 + \Delta t \lambda)^n (u - u_\epsilon)_0.
\end{equation*}
Now if we consider $| (u - u_\epsilon)_n|$ we realize that the 
magnitude of the error will remain controlled as $n \to \infty$ only if 
$| (1 + \Delta t \lambda) | \le 1$. This translates into a restriction
on how large $\Delta t$ can be if we want to control spurious 
oscialltions in our numerical solution.

\chapter{Euler's methods for solving IVPs}

We begin our discussion of numerical algorithms with the 
two simplest and most widely used methods named after 
Leonhard Euler: the forward Euler method and the backward Euler 
method. Throughout this section we consider a first order 
IVP of the form
\begin{equation*}\label{eq:scalar-ivp-lec3}
    \frac{du}{dt} = f(t, u), 
\quad u(0) = c,
\end{equation*}
with the goal of estimating the solution on a finite 
time interval $[0, T]$ for some $T > 0$. The ideas we develop 
here can be immediately extended to systems of ODEs and 
hence higher order problems as well. 

The first question we need to address, before even considering 
a numerical approximation of the solution $u(t)$ is 
whether this problem is well-posed to begin with. In other words, 
is the analytic problem solvable in a reasonable sense? for this 
we adopt the sense of well-posedness introduced by 
Jacques Hadamard \cite[Ch.~1]{higham2015princeton}: 

\begin{quote}
    A mathematical problem is well-posed if it satisfies the following three conditions:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item A solution exists.
        \item The solution is unique.
        \item The solution's behavior changes continuously 
        with the data.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{quote}

In the case of ODEs this definition translates to the 
existence and uniqueness of the solution $u(t)$ as well as 
its continuous dependence on the initial data $u_0$. Intuitively,
this continuous dependence means that if we perturb $u_0$ 
slightly then the solution trajector $u(t)$ will also 
change only slightly. The precise statement is as follows. See 
\cite[Sec.~2.2 and 2.3]{perko2013differential} for details or 
any other classic text on ODEs.

\begin{theorem}[Well-posedness of IVPs]
    Consider the IVP 
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{d \ub}{dt} = \fb (t, \ub), \quad \ub(0) = \cb.
    \end{equation*}
    Suppose that $\fb: [0,T] \times \R^d \to \R^d$ 
    is  continuous in its domain and 
     Lipschitz continuous in $\ub$, i.e., 
    there exists a constant $L > 0$ such that 
    \begin{equation*}
        |f (t, \ub) - f(t, \vb)| \le L \|\ub - \vb\|, \quad \forall (t, \ub), (t, \vb) \in [0,T] \times \R^d.
    \end{equation*}
    Then it holds that: 
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item (Existence and uniqueness) 
        There exists a unique solution $\ub(t)$ for any $\ub_0 \in \R^d$.
        
        \item (Continuous dependence on initial data and right hand side) 
           Suppose $\rb: [0,T] \times \R^d \to \R^d$ 
         is a bounded perturbation of $\fb$, i.e., there exists a constant $M > 0$ such that 
         \begin{equation*}
            \|\rb(t, \ub)\| \le M, \quad \forall (t, \ub) \in [0,T] \times \R^d.
         \end{equation*}
         Further let $\hat\ub(t)$ be the solution of the perturbed IVP
         \begin{equation*}
            \frac{d \hat{\ub}}{dt} = \fb (t, \hat{\ub}) 
            + \rb(t, \hat{\ub}), \quad \hat{\ub}(0) = \hat{\cb},
         \end{equation*}
         which we assume exists and is unique. Then we have 
         \begin{equation*}
            \| \ub(t) - \hat{\ub}(t) \| \le 
            e^{Lt} \| \cb - \hat{\cb} \| + 
            \frac{M}{L} (e^{Lt} - 1), \quad \forall t \in [0, T].
         \end{equation*}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}
Note that this theorem is stronger than what is asked for in 
Hadamard notion of well-posedness. Here we treat both the 
initial data and the right hand side as "data" for the problem and 
we obtain not only continuous dependence on these data 
but also Lipschitz continuous dependence.

With piece of mind that the analytic problem is well-posed, we can now 
proceed to obtain numerical approximations of it. 

\section{Euler's methods, a simple application of Taylor's theorem}
With the intuition that IVPs describe the evolution of dynamic quantities 
we may choose to approximate $u(t)$ at a discrete set of points in time. 
Consider a partition of the time interval $[0, T]$ given by
\begin{equation*}
    0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_N = T.
\end{equation*}
Then our goal is to approximate the values $u(t_n)$
with a set of quantities $\{u_n\}_{n=0}^N$, i.e.,  $u_n \approx u(t_n)$
for $n = 0, 1, \dots, N$. 
We often refer to the points 
$t_n$ as the {\it discrete time points} or {\it time mesh points}
or {\it time grid points}. 
We will also write $\delta t_n = t_{n+1} - t_n$
to denote the {\it time step} between consecutive time points also called 
{\it step size}. For simplicty let us assume that $\delta t_n 
= \delta t$ for all $n = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$, i.e., the time steps are 
constant. Such a mesh is called a {\it uniform mesh}.

Recalling Taylor's theorem, we can write 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
            u(t_{n+1}) & = u(t_n) + \frac{du}{dt}(t_n) \delta t + 
    \mathcal{O}(\delta t^2) \\
    & = u(t_n) + f(t_n, u(t_n)) \delta t + \mathcal{O}(\delta t^2) 
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Throwing away the error $\mathcal{O}(\delta t^2)$, 
we obtain the explicit formula:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:explicit_euler}
    u_{n+1} = u_n + f(t_n, u_n) \delta t, \quad n = 1, \dots, N-1, 
    \quad u_0 = c.
\end{equation}
This formula is known as the explicit Euler method. Notice its simplicity: 
we only need to know the current value $u_n$ and the function $f$. We 
then simply plug $u_n$ into $f$ to compute the next value $u_{n+1}$.
In this sense the explicit Euler method truly embodies our 
intuition for "marching forward in time". 
Also observe that we can rewrite the explicit Euler method 
in the more faithful form: 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{u_{n+1} - u_n}{\delta t} = f(t_n, u_n),
\end{equation*}
which reveals the method was obtained using a simple approximation of 
the time derivative $\frac{d u}{dt}$. But, this observation 
readily suggests that other types of approximations 
of the time derivative may give rise to other numerical methods. Then 
one wonders whether any of these schemes are somehow better than others? 
As a simple example, let us rederive Euler's method 
but this time using the Taylor expansion based at $t_{n+1}$ rather 
than $t_n$: 
\begin{equation*}
    \begin{aligned}
    u(t_n) & = u(t_{n+1}) - \frac{du}{dt}(t_{n+1}) \delta t + 
    \mathcal{O}(\delta t^2) \\
    & = u(t_{n+1}) - f(t_{n+1}, u(t_{n+1})) \delta t + \mathcal{O}(\delta t^2)
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
Once again throw away the error term to obtain 
\begin{equation*}
    u_{n+1}  = u_{n} + f(t_{n+1}, u_{n+1}) \delta t, \quad n = 0, \dots, N-1, 
    \quad u_0 = c.
\end{equation*}
Notice that this equation now describes $u_{n+1}$ implicitly 
in terms of $u_n$ at each step, hence it is known as the 
{\it implicit Euler method.}
It's faithful form is 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{u_{n+1} - u_n}{\delta t} = f(t_{n+1}, u_{n+1}). 
\end{equation*}
At this point it seems that the explicit and implicit Euler schemes are 
only slightly different with the implicit scheme being less convenient 
to use: notice that to compute $u_{n+1}$ we now need to solve a 
possibly nonlinear equation at each step. However, as we will see 
below, there is a profound difference between the two methods. 

\section{Comparing the explicit and implicit Euler methods on 
the test ODE}
Consider the following ODE, which we will refer to as the {\it 
test ODE}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:test_ode}
    \frac{du}{dt} = \lambda u, \quad u(0) = c, \quad t > 0.
\end{equation}
for some constant $\lambda \in \C$, we allow complex values since 
this becomes important when we deal with systems. The solution 
is simply given by $u(t) = c e^{\lambda t}$. Now let us solve 
this ODE using our two methods. 


The companion notebook for this lecture can be used to reproduce the 
results in \Cref{fig:test_ode_forward_backward_euler_comparison}.
Here we take $\lambda = -0.8 + 0.2i$ and $c = 1$. On the left column of 
the figure we plot the analytic solution against the forward and backward Euler 
solutions on the interval $[0, 10]$ with step size $\delta t = 0.5$. 
We observe a gap between the numerical solutions 
and the analytic one, this is expected since we are only solving the ODE approximately.
 However, the solutions remain close to the analytic one and their qualitative behavior 
 matches the truth. When using step size $\delta t = 2.2$ the picture changes 
 significantly. The forward Euler solution exhibits large oscillations while 
 the backward method seems to remain more or less {\it stable}. 
 
 In the following lectures we will formalize this idea of stability for 
 a numerical algorithm but the behavior seen in \Cref{fig:test_ode_forward_backward_euler_comparison} is prototypical for 
 explicit and implicit numerical methods: {\it explicit methods are often cheaper 
 per iteration but they can be unstable, whereas implicit methods are often 
 more expensive but they tend to be more stable.}
